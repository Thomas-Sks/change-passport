The Change Passport: concept card

Tieto challenge "Earned Trust in AI Code", Junction 2026

Recommendation

Don't ask reviewers to read agent code faster: change what they review. Every agent-written change ships with a passport that states what was asked, what the agent claims, an executed proof for each claim (before vs after, in a sandbox), what the AI refused to decide, and how much a human must read. A named owner reads only what the risk requires and signs a bounded attestation. The AI advises with evidence. It never approves.

The LLM proposes, execution proves, rules decide, a named human signs.

Change (code + requirement ID + agent claims) → Sort (zones, traceability, orphan code) → Prove (sandbox, base vs branch, 3 runs) → AI review (blind to proofs, then verified against them) → Lane (green / orange / red, explicit rules) → Owner signs (bound to the commit)

Principles

  1. Requirement first: short acceptance criteria locked by a human before review.
  2. Evidence, not confidence: a claim counts only if an executed proof supports it.
  3. Independence from the agent: the agent's own tests are the weakest evidence.
  4. Rules decide the lane: the AI can raise risk, never lower it.
  5. Review depth follows risk: a human signs in every lane, even green.
  6. Refusing is a normal output: API, user-visible behaviour, compatibility and risk acceptance go to a person.
  7. Trust is earned per zone: it is lightened by track record and suspended after an escape.
  8. The factory secures itself: PR content is untrusted input, and no AI has merge rights.

How it balances the five tensions

Tension Our answer
Risk Explainable rules route attention: green 1–2 min, orange reads a listed part, red goes back to the agent
Hallucination Every AI finding must quote code and is checked against proofs: confirmed, rejected, or opinion — proofs disagree
Transparency The passport says what was not checked and why; every proof replays with one command
Confidence No model-stated confidence is used. Evidence sets severity
Quality Mutation testing measures the agent's tests; catching tests come from the criteria, not from the agent

What the owner signs

Checked (by whom or what) · Not checked (why) · a decision on each AI refusal · residual risk accepted · bound to the commit and the passport fingerprint. The signing step itself refuses the wrong signer, a newer commit, unanswered refusals, and a red change accepted without a full read.

How trust is measured

Reverts and incidents per lane and zone · random counter-review of 10% of green changes · canary bugs injected continuously · share of AI findings confirmed by evidence · time to merge per lane · share routed to humans. Circuit breakers are set in advance: an escape suspends its zone, and a canary passing green suspends the green lane.

Trade-offs and limits

  • Cost: humans write 3–6 criteria per change, and each repository needs one end-to-end harness per product type.
  • Friction: a signature even in green. A false orange costs a review; a false green costs an incident.
  • Bounded by the criteria: a wrong criterion gives a passport that is proven and wrong.
  • Not judged: architecture and product intent. They become refusals, that is, human decisions.
  • Without agent claims and harnesses, the passport still routes and records accountability, but the owner reads everything.

The judges' questions

  • Who owns the decision? The named CODEOWNER signs and merges. The policy guardian owns the rules. The AI owns nothing.
  • What would make us distrust our own output? A canary passing green, a revert linked to a green passport, counter-review agreement dropping, or signatures faster than the required reading.
  • How does it scale? Reading grows with risk, not diff size (3–17% of lines on our samples). Proofs run in parallel on existing CI runners, and the chain takes minutes per change.

The Change Passport

How can a named human accept code written by an AI agent — without reading all of it — and still be accountable for it? Tieto challenge "Earned Trust in AI Code" — Junction 2026

The idea in 30 seconds

Agents now write code faster than humans can read it. Reading every line no longer scales, and letting an AI approve removes accountability.

We propose a different object of review: the Change Passport. Every agent-written change travels with a passport that says what was asked, what the agent claims it did, the proof for each claim — obtained by running the code before and after the change, not by asking an AI's opinion — what the AI refused to decide, and how much a human must read. A named owner reads only what the risk requires, can replay any proof with one command, and signs what was checked and what was not.

The AI reviews. The proofs decide what is true. A human decides and stays accountable.

How it works

Step What happens Why
1 · Change The agent delivers code + the requirement ID. A human has locked 3–6 short acceptance criteria; the agent declares its claims. Every check needs a reference for "correct" that does not come from the agent itself.
2 · Sort A versioned policy sorts files: generated, normal, sensitive (public API, build, security). Each change is linked to a criterion; code linked to nothing is orphan and always shown. Not all lines deserve the same attention; rules that anyone can read decide, not a black box.
3 · Prove In a disposable sandbox, the change runs before and after, 3 times: end-to-end on the real product, catching tests generated from the criteria, mutation testing (do the tests even notice each change?), regeneration of generated files. Reading shows what code says; running shows what it does. The agent's own tests are not independent evidence.
4 · AI review A reviewer model (different from the author) lists findings, each quoting the code, then checked against the proofs. It refuses decisions that belong to people (public API, user-visible behaviour, compatibility, risk acceptance). AI reviewers are useful but often wrong; an unproven finding is labelled "opinion".
5 · Lane Explicit rules choose green (quick check), orange (read the listed parts) or red (send back or full read). The AI can raise the risk, never lower it. Human attention goes where the risk is; every routing decision is explainable.
6 · Owner signs The owner answers two questions before seeing the AI's view, reads what the lane lists, decides each refusal, replays any proof, and signs: checked (by whom/what) · not checked (why) · risk accepted. The owner merges. A bounded signature makes accountability real without asking anyone to "have read everything".

Trust is then measured (reverts, incidents, random counter-reviews, injected canary bugs) and earned per code zone: a zone with a clean track record gets lighter review; one escape suspends it.

Why we designed it this way

  1. Review is now the bottleneck. Teams adopting AI see review time ×5 and 31% more PRs merged with no review at all (Faros 2026). Tieto's own Agentic DevOps doubled delivery — and doubled the review load.
  2. Human review was already weaker than we assume. At Vercel, over half of PRs were approved without a comment, 18% in under 5 minutes. A click is not a check.
  3. AI reviewers alone are not trustworthy. The best reach 50–60% F1 on real PRs, are confidently wrong, and favour code from their own model family.
  4. An agent's tests prove what the agent believes, not what was asked. 80% of agent-written tests have weak or no assertions. In our own run, an agent's change passed all 169 tests and still broke users' builds.
  5. Auto-approval solves speed, not accountability. Meta, Vercel or Intercom auto-approve low-risk PRs, but none says who answers when an auto-approved change breaks.

So the design keeps three things separate: the requirement (human), the evidence (machine, independent of the agent) and the decision (named human).

What it brings

For Benefit
The owner Reads a fraction of the change (one 237-file agent change: 46 of 1,392 lines), with the most dangerous parts first; sees proofs, not opinions; can replay any of them.

On one of them the run also showed the issue's own description was inaccurate (the analyzer crashed rather than "did not work").

In the live run the AI raised a user-visible question nobody had settled, and the rules moved the change from orange to red: the AI can raise risk, never lower it.

1. The problem

What changed

Agents now write a large share of the code. In 2026 the bottleneck is no longer writing code, it is verifying it.

Observation Source
Review time ×5, incidents per PR +243%, 31% more PRs merged with no review at all, in teams adopting AI heavily Faros AI, Acceleration Whiplash (2026, 22,000 developers, vendor data)
AI-written code receives 1.9× more blocking review threads Google, 3.5 M C++ changes (arXiv 2608.06640, 2026)
Most agent-written PRs receive no human review at all; when reviewed, it is mostly by other agents AIDev study (EASE 2026, arXiv 2605.02273)
Tieto's own Agentic DevOps doubled delivery productivity, with a human approving every merge Tieto, Agentic DevOps with GitHub Copilot (Sept. 2026)

Doubling the output of agents doubles the review load. Human review becomes the constraint.

Why line-by-line review is not the answer

  1. It does not scale. Detection collapses beyond roughly 150–400 changed lines per review; agent changes routinely exceed that (one of the challenge branches changes 237 files).
  2. It was already weaker than we think. Before automation, Vercel found that more than half of its PRs were approved without a single comment and 18% in under 5 minutes. A human click is often a rubber stamp.
  3. Reading is not checking. A reviewer reading complex code labelled "AI" accepts more incorrect changes (controlled study, 385 reviewers, FSE 2026).

Why "let the AI approve" is not the answer either

  1. AI reviewers miss a lot. The best tools reach 50–60% F1 on real pull requests (Martian Code Review Bench, 2026), and almost nothing beyond ~150 changed lines (arXiv 2606.15689).
  2. They are confidently wrong. Explanations raise human confidence without raising correctness (arXiv 2607.08885, 2026).
  3. A model judges its own family favourably (+3 to +8 points, arXiv 2609.17857) and corrects its own errors far less than others' (arXiv 2606.05976).
  4. The agent's own tests prove little. 80% of tests written by agents have weak or no oracles (All Smoke, No Alarm, arXiv 2606.18168); about 1 in 5 "resolved" benchmark patches is actually wrong (SWE-ABS, arXiv 2603.00520).
  5. Accountability disappears. Companies that auto-approve low-risk changes (Meta RADAR, Vercel, Intercom) publish speed and revert numbers, but none states who answers when an auto-approved change breaks — the person nearest to the failure ends up blamed for something they did not control (the "moral crumple zone").

The design goal

A named human can accept an agent-written change without reading all of it, because evidence traceable to the requirement shows what was checked, and the system says plainly what was not checked and what the AI refused to decide.

Three questions every change must answer:

  1. Does it do what was asked? (requirement)
  2. What could break? (risk)
  3. Who answers for it? (accountability)

Three lanes — a human signs in every lane

Lane When Human work Typical time
Green Only mechanical/ordinary code in a trusted zone, every claim proven, no refusal Read the passport summary, sign. 10% of greens are drawn at random for a full counter-review 1–2 min
Orange Sensitive zone, unproven claim, orphan code, zone not yet trusted Answer two questions, read the hunks in the reading contract, decide refusals, sign 10–30 min

What is deterministic, what is not

The design keeps every non-deterministic part advisory and every decision deterministic and explainable.

Part Deterministic? Consequence
Sorting into zones, traceability, reading contract Yes: versioned rules Same change → same reading contract
Executed evidence (base vs branch) Made stable: 3 runs per side; any difference → "unstable", never counts as proof A flaky test cannot create a fake catch
AI review (findings, refusals) No: two runs on the same change can differ Each finding is checked against the proofs: quote not found → rejected; contradicted by a proof → "opinion — proofs disagree"; unverified → "opinion"
Lane Yes: rules over evidence + refusals The AI can only add refusals, so a different AI run can raise the lane, never lower it
Signature Human Bound to the commit and to the passport fingerprint

Observed: on one change, a live AI run raised a user-visible refusal that a previous run did not, and the lane went from orange to red. We did not tune the rules to get the earlier lane back: the variance can only cost a review, not let a change through.

Measured on our reference implementation (one laptop, warm build): the whole chain on a 4-file bug fix took about 2–3 minutes, most of it the base/branch runs.

Where it plugs into an existing pipeline

The passport is an extra layer between "the agent is done" and "a human approves". It replaces nothing.

  • Merge gate: an external status check (GitLab) or a required check (GitHub) that stays red until the attestation is signed by the CODEOWNER of the touched zones.
  • On-premises: every component runs locally (scripts, git, the project's own build tool); no SaaS is required — a hard constraint for regulated or mission-critical teams such as Tieto's telecom product teams.
  • Jira: the requirement ID and the hash of the locked criteria are stored on the story; the signed attestation is attached to the merge request.

Step 1 — Requirement, locked criteria, agent claims

A human locks 3–6 short acceptance criteria; the agent declares one-sentence claims; both go into the passport.

1.1 Locked acceptance criteria

What it does. Before (or right after) the agent is assigned, a human writes 3–6 short acceptance criteria for the requirement, e.g. "Generated code still compiles for every existing valid user project." The criteria are stored with a hash; the agent cannot change them. Every later step checks the change against these criteria, not against what the agent says.

Why this choice.

  • Every check needs an oracle: something that says what "correct" means. If the oracle comes from the agent (its tests, its summary), the check proves what the agent believes, not what was asked. LLM-generated test oracles tend to mirror the code's actual behaviour rather than the expected behaviour (arXiv 2410.21136).
  • Validating a link between a requirement and code is far more reliable than discovering it (98.9% vs 85.5% on automotive requirements, TVR, arXiv 2504.15427). Short criteria written by a human give the machine something to validate against.
  • Formal verification work shows agents sometimes weaken the specification instead of fixing the code (Vero benchmark, arXiv 2608.13522). Locking the criteria prevents that.

Why short. Long specifications do not buy trust: a Spec Kit experience report produced 2,577 lines of markdown for one feature, 3h30 of review instead of 15 minutes, and still missed an obvious bug (Scott Logic, Nov. 2025). We aim for criteria a reviewer can read in one minute.

Alternatives rejected.

  • Use the issue text as is — prose cannot be checked by a machine.
  • Let the agent write the criteria — the agent would grade its own homework.
  • Full spec-driven development (Kiro, Spec Kit) — useful upstream, but too verbose to be the review contract.

Limits. A badly written criterion gives a passport that is "proven" and still wrong. Criteria cost human time; this is the main price of the concept.

Generic or per repository? Generic. Fits existing flows: at Tieto the Product Owner already approves a requirements document before stories are created — the criteria are extracted from it.

1.2 Agent claims

What it does. The agent declares, one sentence each, what it claims to have done ("All generated formatters carry the attribute", "No behaviour change at runtime") and which criterion each claim serves.

Why this choice.

  • A claim is something that can be proven or contradicted. A free-form summary cannot.
  • Claims make orphan code visible: code that serves no claim and no criterion is the classic agent drift (unrequested refactors, build tweaks).
  • Agents already produce this material: Tieto's planning agent writes a "standard tracking file"; it becomes the claim list.

Alternatives rejected. Trusting the agent's completion report — the author summarising its own work is self-assessment, exactly what the challenge asks us to avoid.

Limits. An agent can write vague claims to "cover" everything. That is why links are checked independently (Step 2) and claims only count when evidence supports them (Step 3).

Generic or per repository? Generic; requires the agent prompt/template to emit claims.

Step 2 — Split the change and build the reading contract

The diff is sorted by a versioned policy; generated files are regenerated and compared; moved code is detected; each hunk is linked to a criterion. What remains becomes the reading contract, sorted by risk, with the list of what is excluded and why.

2.1 Policy zones

What it does. A versioned policy file (owned by the policy guardian, protected like CODEOWNERS) classifies every changed file: mechanical (generated), ordinary, sensitive (public API, build/CI, agent instruction files, security, data migrations), tests, docs. Sensitive zones have a severity (minor/major).

Why this choice.

  • Every company that auto-approves part of its PRs starts with hard rules the AI cannot override: CODEOWNERS paths, size limits, never-auto-approve zones (Vercel, Rewind, Ona, Meta RADAR — 2026 engineering blogs).
  • A rule is explainable: "this file is in the public-API zone" is a reason anyone can check. The challenge explicitly rejects "a risk score nobody can explain".
  • The LLM may raise a zone's risk, never lower it (Vercel, Rewind).

Limits found by research. A path is not a risk: critical logic in an "ordinary" file that feeds the public API slips through. Target improvement: raise a hunk's level when a changed symbol is used by a sensitive zone (call graph or symbol search, as Meta's Diff Risk Score uses change features).

Generic or per repository? Mechanism generic; zone content is per repository (like CODEOWNERS).

2.2 Generated and derived files

What it does. Files produced by a tool (snapshots, generated code, lockfiles) are regenerated in the sandbox from their sources and compared with the committed version. If they match, they are proven without being read. Any difference not reproduced by regeneration goes to the reading contract. Derived files (e.g. code produced from a template) are read through their source.

Why this choice. Agent changes are often dominated by generated output (one challenge branch: 222 of 237 files). Reading them is wasted attention; trusting them blindly is dangerous, because an agent can regenerate snapshots to make a wrong behaviour "green". Regeneration turns them into evidence.

Alternative rejected. Collapsing generated files (GitHub linguist-generated) — hides them without proving them.

Generic or per repository? Generic; the regeneration command is per repository.

2.3 Moved code

What it does. A block deleted in one place and added identically elsewhere in the same diff is a move, not new logic. It is excluded from reading, with the proof (source location) shown.

Why. Moves otherwise count twice (deleted + added) and inflate the reading contract; Devin Review and GitHub's stacked-PR guidance treat moved code separately (2026).

2.4 Links between code and criteria

What it does (target form). An independent model, which does not see the agent's claims, proposes for each changed hunk the criterion it serves, with a one-line justification. The proposal is compared with the agent's claims:

  • agreement → linked;
  • disagreement → risk signal shown to the owner;
  • no criterion → orphan code, always in the reading contract. The owner confirms or rejects links in one click.

Why this choice.

  • Keyword matching lets the agent write its own traceability (a broad keyword links everything) and misses synonyms.
  • LLM traceability recovery has improved (R2Code, F1 0.73, arXiv 2604.22432) but is still a proposal, not a proof — hence human confirmation.
  • Linking hunk → criterion (not hunk → claim) removes the agent from the chain.

Limit. In our reference implementation, links are still made by keyword patterns per claim. We present the target form and state this gap.

2.5 The reading contract

What it does. The list of hunks the owner must read, sorted by risk (orphan code and files breaking a rule first, then weak tests, then sensitive zones), plus a table of what is excluded and why (rule, proof).

Why this choice.

  • Order matters: a defect placed in the last file of a review has 64% lower odds of being found (controlled experiment with 106 developers, Fregnan et al., FSE 2022); defect rates rise with file position across 330,000 PRs (Rahman et al., 2026).
  • Showing exclusions prevents false assurance: "read 46 lines" means nothing if the filter silently dropped a dangerous hunk.

Generic or per repository? Generic.

Step 3 — Independent evidence

The core idea: reading tells you what code says; running it tells you what it does. Every piece of evidence runs on the base (before the change) and the branch (after), in a disposable sandbox, and can be replayed with one command.

3.1 End-to-end execution in a sandbox

What it does. The change is built and run as a user would: a library is consumed by a real project per target; a service is started with an ephemeral database and called; a UI is driven in a browser. Each criterion gets passed / failed / not tested with artifacts (logs, traces, screenshots, video).

Why. An agent fails differently from a human: its tests and the existing CI can all be green while users break. OpenAI (Codex tasks in isolated containers, summaries citing test logs), Minitap (mobile QA agent, expected-vs-observed per criterion), Greptile TREX, Devin and Cursor (agents in VMs returning videos) all moved to execution evidence in 2026.

Rule. A green end-to-end run proves only the criteria it covers, and only if its scenarios were derived from criteria signed beforehand. "Not tested" stays visible and never counts as success.

Generic or per repository? Principle generic; one harness per product type (library, service, UI) — a real setup cost.

3.2 Catching tests generated from the criteria

What it does. A separate model reads only the criteria and the list of touched files (not the agent's code comments or tests) and writes disposable scenarios. Each runs on base and branch. Pass → fail is a catch; fail → pass proves implementation; fail → fail goes to a human (criterion not met, or invalid test). Tests are never committed.

Why. Meta's Just-in-Time tests (FSE 2026): diff-aware generated tests found 4× more candidate bugs than hardening tests; in production, 60% true positives, 74 serious bugs blocked in H1 2026, −20% incidents. Humans stay on the loop: they set criteria and review aggregated results.

Why the generator must not read the agent's comments. If it does, it inherits the agent's mistakes (we observed exactly this: a generated scenario repeated the agent's false premise).

3.3 Mutation testing scoped to the diff

What it does. Each change of the agent is reverted one at a time (and, in the target form, finer mutants are injected on changed lines, e.g. Stryker for .NET); we record which mutants the delivered tests notice. A change no test notices pushes the lane to orange.

Why. Coverage says code was executed, not checked. Google shows diff-scoped mutants in code review at scale (~17 M mutants, arXiv 2102.11378); Meta's ACH generates tests that kill surviving mutants (73% accepted by engineers).

Cost control. N mutants × test suite is expensive → only in orange/red lanes.

3.4 Guards on the evidence itself

Guard Why
3 runs per side; a changing result is "unstable" and never proof A flaky test would create fake catches (Meta TestGen-LLM keeps only stable tests)
Assertion strength check: no assertion, trivial assertion, tautology (Count >= 0), text-only checks are flagged The weak link of LLM-written tests is the oracle, not coverage (Ernst et al., ASE 2025)
Test ↔ criterion link: each test cites the criterion it covers; a criterion is "supported" only if a linked test passes A link is not a proof; only a linked, passing execution is (ReqToCode, 2026)
Environment claims are evidence too: if the agent asserts something about the platform ("X is not available on target Y"), it must be checked Agents encode false premises in code, docs and tests at once

3.5 One-command replay

What it does. Every executed evidence can be re-run by the owner on base and branch, side by side; the scenario is editable to explore; each replay is recorded in the attestation.

Why. "Trust is built on evidence a person can check and own" (challenge brief). A proof you can replay yourself is one you can sign. Replay.io's "rerecording" (same interactions before/after) is the closest industry practice.

Generic or per repository? Generic; uses the same harnesses as 3.1–3.3.

Steps 4 & 5 — AI review and the refusal register

4. AI review

What it does. A reviewer model reads the change with full repository context and the criteria. It must quote the diff before concluding, classify each hunk on a closed grid (safe categories vs risk signals — one risk signal escalates), and list what it refuses to decide. A refuter tries to disprove each finding; surviving findings are checked against executed evidence. No style or naming remarks.

Why each design choice.

Choice Reason Source
Different model family from the author Judges favour their own family (+3 to +8 pts); models correct others' errors far better than their own arXiv 2609.17857; arXiv 2606.05976
Quote before concluding Forces grounding in the code; Vercel's classifier must list evidence quotes before its verdict Vercel engineering blog (Apr. 2026)
Closed grid, one risk signal escalates Turns the LLM into a classifier, not an essayist; Meta RADAR accepts only if every hunk is in a safe category Meta RADAR (arXiv 2605.30208)
Refuter before publication Adversarial agents removed ~80% of candidate findings before release; watch for false consensus Refute-or-Promote (arXiv 2604.19049)
Checked against evidence AI reviewers reach 50–60% F1 on real PRs; precision first or developers stop reading Martian Bench 2026; Uber uReview
No style remarks Style noise is the first reason teams switch off AI reviewers Graphite, Cloudflare "What NOT to flag"

What the AI review is not. It is advice with evidence, never the approval (challenge brief). An unconfirmed finding is labelled "opinion" and cannot block alone; an empty AI review is not a green light.

Generic or per repository? Generic.

5. The refusal register

What it does. A catalogue of decision types the AI never takes, even when confident. Each refusal names the question, the reason, and the person who decides:

Decision type Why the AI refuses Default decider
Public API shape and naming Irreversible once published API owner
New public identifiers (diagnostic IDs, error codes) Permanent Maintainer
User-visible behaviour change Product intent Owner
Compatibility / supported targets scope Product and support commitment Owner
Accepting a security risk Accountability Owner + security
Any claim without available evidence "I cannot prove it" is an answer Owner

Refusals are raised from three sources only: policy zones (e.g. public-API zone touched), evidence (e.g. an execution shows user-visible behaviour changed), and the AI reviewer. A major refusal forces the red lane.

Why.

  • The challenge asks to show what the AI refused to decide, and why.
  • Every vendor admits the same blind spots — architecture, cross-system effects, product intent (Cloudflare, Intercom, Mary Shaw keynote 2026) — so they must be routed, not guessed.
  • An AI that never refuses anything is a warning sign, not a strength (see measurement).

Securing the factory itself

The AI reviewer reads attacker-controllable content (PR titles, comments, instruction files, images). 2026 incidents: CI secrets exfiltrated through PR titles via AI review actions (Comment and Control, 2 CVEs), an AI reviewer auto-approving malicious code because of a spoofed Git identity, hidden instructions in images (GhostCommit).

Rule Effect
PR content is untrusted data Never treated as instructions
Reviewer is read-only, no secrets, no outbound network A hijacked reviewer can only produce a passport, which a human reads
No approval and no merge rights for any AI Approval stays human by construction
Declared author identity never lowers risk Defeats identity spoofing
Changes to CI, build scripts and agent instruction files are a sensitive zone These are the 2026 attack paths
Evidence runs in a disposable sandbox PR-provided configs cannot touch the factory

Steps 6 & 7 — Lanes and the signature

6. Lanes decided by rules

What it does. A deterministic verdict engine (ordered rules, testable code — not a model) turns evidence, zones, refusals and the trust register into a lane, and lists every reason in plain words.

Why.

  • Rewind's "Diff Vader": specialised LLM reviewers produce findings, "ordinary, unit-tested code, not a model" decides. Meta RADAR: conjunctive funnel where any failure goes back to a human.
  • Asymmetry by design: a false orange costs one review; a false green lets risky code ship (Vercel).
  • Thoughtworks Technology Radar (vol. 35, Nov. 2026) puts risk-tiered human oversight in Trial and warns against "human-in-the-loop theater" where everything gets the same attention.

Difference with industry practice. Meta, Vercel, Intercom and Rewind let the AI approve the low-risk tier. We do not: green still requires a light human signature, because the challenge requires a named human per change and because no company publishes who answers when an auto-approved change breaks.

Trust earned per zone

State Rule
New Default; no green lane in this zone
Trusted ≥ N signed changes over 60 days, 0 reverts/incidents, ≥ 90% agreement in random counter-review
Suspended Any revert or incident linked to a passport of the zone; the factory gets a new rule or test for what it missed, then the zone requalifies

Why. Meta RADAR grants autonomy per agent "recipe" from 60 days of history, with daily caps and a denylist after incidents. The challenge brief: "Trust is earned per area, from track record, and withdrawn after escapes."

7. The signature

What it does.

  1. Two questions before the AI view (orange/red): Do the criteria reflect the issue? What must not change for users? The page hides the AI analysis until they are answered.
  2. The owner reads the reading contract, ticks each hunk, decides each refusal.
  3. The owner signs a bounded attestation:
Section Content
Checked — by whom or what each item with its source: rule, sandbox run, AI + verification, "me (reading)"
Not checked — why everything without evidence, with the reason
Decisions each refusal and the owner's decision
Residual risk accepted in the owner's words
Identity name, date, passport fingerprint, commit
  1. The owner merges. The signature is bound to the commit: a new commit invalidates it.

Why each choice.

Choice Reason Source
Questions before the AI view Displaying an AI review narrows reviewers' attention; explanations raise acceptance whether the AI is right or wrong; cognitive forcing reduces over-reliance Tufano ICSE 2025; Bansal CHI 2021 (confirmed 2026, arXiv 2607.08885); Buçinca CSCW 2021
Bounded attestation, not "approve" Avoids the moral crumple zone; the owner answers for what they saw, not for everything Elish 2019; challenge brief "What the owner signs"
Owner merges, not the bot "Human merge authority is the trust anchor" (Ona); auto-approve is not auto-merge (Rewind); compliance needs an auditable change process Ona, Rewind, Vercel (2026)
Signature bound to the commit You cannot sign a version other than the one reviewed CodeRabbit Change Stack, Devin Review (2026)

Limits. Friction: 1–2 minutes even in green, more in orange. We reserve the questions for orange and red.

4. Principles and trade-offs

How the design balances the five forces of the challenge

Force Risk if ignored How the passport handles it
Risk Same attention for a typo and a public-API change → rubber stamp Policy zones + lanes + trust per zone; depth of human review follows risk
Hallucination AI invents bugs (noise) or denies real ones (false reassurance) Findings must quote the diff, survive a refuter and be confirmed by execution; unconfirmed = "opinion"; an empty AI review is never a green light
Transparency Nobody can explain why a change was routed or accepted Deterministic verdict engine with written reasons; reading contract lists what is excluded and why; every proof replayable
Confidence Trust in a score or in the AI's tone No confidence score is shown; only evidence statuses (proven / contradicted / not tested); trust is earned per zone from outcomes
Quality Green tests, broken users Evidence independent of the agent: criteria as oracle, base-vs-branch execution, end-to-end runs, mutation, assertion checks

The eight principles and the reasoning behind each

Principle Reasoning What would break without it
1. Requirement first Every check needs an oracle; the agent cannot be its own oracle Checks prove what the agent believes
2. Evidence, not confidence AI reviewers reach 50–60% F1; explanations raise confidence, not correctness "The AI said it was fine"
3. Independence Same-family judging and self-correction are biased; 80% of agent tests have weak oracles Agent tests green, users broken
4. Rules decide the lane Explainable, testable, auditable; the AI can only add risk Opaque risk scores, silent downgrades
5. Depth follows risk Human attention is the scarce resource; uniform review becomes a stamp Either everything is read (no scale) or nothing is (no trust)
6. Refusing is normal Architecture, product intent, compatibility are human decisions The AI quietly decides product questions
7. Trust per zone Autonomy must be earned and revocable (Meta per-recipe history) One bad zone poisons trust everywhere, or nothing ever gets lighter
8. The factory secures itself The reviewer reads hostile input A hijacked reviewer approves or leaks secrets

Explicit trade-offs

Decision What we gain What we give up
Human signs even green changes Named accountability for every change (challenge requirement) 1–2 minutes per change; less speed than Meta/Vercel auto-approval
Criteria written by humans An independent oracle Human time upfront; quality depends on the criteria
Lean to orange when in doubt Fewer escaped defects More reviews early on, until zones earn trust
Execution-based evidence Proof instead of opinion One harness per repository/product type; compute cost
Mutation only in orange/red Bounded cost Green changes rely on other evidence
Questions before the AI view Less automation bias Friction; users rate such interfaces lower (Buçinca 2021)
Precision over recall in AI findings Owners keep reading them Some real issues are not raised by the AI — hence execution evidence
No AI approval at all Accountability by construction We do not reach the 40–75% "no human" rates published by some companies

5. Measuring trust

The judges' question: do reverts and incidents stay flat while review time falls; are AI findings real and accepted; what is time-to-merge; what share goes to humans? Trust must be measured, and lighter review must be earned.

Progressive activation

Inspired by Vercel (shadow → visible → enforced, with kill switches defined in advance: reverts > 3× baseline, rollbacks > 3× baseline, negative team sentiment) and Rewind (simulation on ~3,000 historical PRs, one week in shadow, activation above 90% agreement with humans).

Dashboard

Judges' question Metric Why this metric
Do reverts/incidents rise as review time falls? Reverts and incidents per lane and per zone, linked back to the passport; time spent per lane Aggregate numbers hide where trust is misplaced
Is the comparison fair? Random counter-review: 10% of green changes drawn at random and fully reviewed; agreement rate All published industry baselines (Meta, Vercel, Intercom) are non-randomised and biased by selection
Are AI findings real and accepted? % confirmed by evidence; % followed by a fix; "Incorrect?" clicks Industry reference: 36–39% of AI comments lead to a change vs 44% for humans (Atlassian, CodeRabbit study)
What does the factory miss? Canary bugs injected continuously from a library of typical agent defects: % caught, per lane The only way to measure recall without waiting for incidents
Are tests strong? Mutation score of delivered tests; share of criteria with a passing linked test Coverage and green tests say little about agent code
Are results stable? Share of unstable evidence over 3 runs Flaky evidence is not evidence
Time to merge p50 / p75 per lane; signature time measured, not declared Developers overestimate AI speed-ups (METR)
Share routed to humans Green / orange / red split; lines to read ÷ lines changed Shows where attention goes
Is the human really reading? Required hunks opened; signatures faster than the reading time required Detects rubber-stamping

Circuit breakers (defined before activation)

  • A revert or incident linked to a passport → its zone is suspended.
  • Counter-review agreement < 90% → green lane suspended globally.
  • A canary bug passes as green → green lane suspended; the factory gets a new rule or test.

What would make us distrust our own output

A canary passing green · a revert linked to a green passport · counter-review agreement dropping · signatures faster than the required reading · unstable evidence growing · an AI that never refuses anything · links that the owner keeps rejecting.

What our reference implementation showed (illustration, not statistics)

Observation Value
On one 237-file agent change, lines a human had to read 46 of 1,392 (3%) — the rest proven by regeneration or read through their source
Same change: agent's own tests all green; they noticed 3 of the agent's 10 changes under mutation
Same change: independent evidence 2 regressions for users found by execution against the criteria
AI reviewer, without seeing our evidence 4 findings, all confirmed; 1 real issue missed

6. Limits and failure modes

What is generic, what must be configured per repository

Component Generic Per repository Per requirement
Locked criteria, agent claims ✓ criteria text
Policy zones, lanes, verdict engine ✓ (mechanism) zone list (like CODEOWNERS)
Regenerate & compare generated files ✓ regeneration command
End-to-end sandbox ✓ (principle) one harness per product type scenarios derived from criteria
Catching tests, mutation, replay ✓ test runner + harness template
Assertion-strength check ✓ (principle) per language / test framework
AI review, refusal register, signature, trust per zone ✓

Honest cost statement: the concept saves reading time only where a repository has invested in harnesses and where agents declare claims. Without them, the passport still routes and records accountability, but the owner reads everything.

What we removed from the workflow because it was over-fitted

While building the reference implementation we wrote some checks for one specific change. We removed them from the concept and kept only their generic principle:

Over-fitted check Generic principle kept
A regex listing the exact lines allowed in generated snapshots Regenerate generated files and compare
A check that every generated type carries one specific attribute It is just an acceptance criterion turned into a test
Refusals and "not checked" items written for one change Refusals come only from policy, evidence and the AI reviewer
Inspecting .NET reference assemblies to disprove one agent statement Agent claims about the environment are claims to prove
A bench of 8 bugs written for one change Continuous canary bugs from a library of typical agent defects

Limits of the concept

  • Criteria quality bounds everything: a wrong criterion yields a passport that is proven and wrong.
  • Architecture, cross-system effects, product intent are not judged — they become refusals.
  • Traceability is a proposal: LLM link recovery is still imperfect (state of the art F1 ≈ 0.73), so links need human confirmation; our reference implementation still links by keywords.
  • End-to-end evidence proves only what it covers; untested scenarios stay "not tested".
  • Same model family risk: if author and reviewer share a family, bias returns; the family of the author is not always known.
  • Industry numbers we cite are mostly self-reported and selection-biased; we rely on random counter-review for our own measurement.

Failure modes we observed (in agent code and in our own factory)

9. Adopting the passport on an existing project

Short answer: yes — the passport is designed as an additive layer on top of the pull-request flow a project already has. It consumes what the existing pipeline already produces (CI results, test suites, CODEOWNERS, branch protection, issue tracker) and adds what is missing (independent evidence, refusals, lanes, a bounded signature). Nothing existing is removed, and nothing changes for the team until the passport is activated zone by zone.

What the passport reuses from the existing project

Existing test suite Run on base and branch; target of mutation testing; source of "fail before / pass after"
Snapshot / golden tests Act as the "regenerate & compare" proof for generated files
Test helpers and verifiers Declared once in the policy so the assertion check recognises them
CODEOWNERS Gives the named owner per zone and seeds the policy's sensitive zones
Branch protection / required approvals Becomes the merge gate: an extra required check "attestation signed"

  • For new work, a human writes 3–6 acceptance criteria; legacy requirements are not back-filled.
  • Owners sign an attestation instead of clicking "approve".

What our reference implementation shows about brownfield use

The demo repository is an existing, mature open-source project (MessagePack-CSharp: CI on GitHub Actions and Azure Pipelines, ~170 source-generator tests with a snapshot verifier). We did not modify its code, tests or infrastructure:

  • its existing test suite served as regeneration and base/branch evidence;
  • its own test verifier helpers were declared in the policy instead of being rewritten;
  • the issue's own reproduction became an end-to-end proof without new infrastructure.

Demonstrated in a real pull request (one private repository): the passport posted as a PR comment, a passport/owner-signature commit status pending until the named owner signs, and the signed attestation bound to the PR head commit.

Not demonstrated: an enforced merge gate (the status is not a required check in our demo repository), reusing the project's CI results instead of re-running them, and container-based sandboxes — our sandboxes were isolated git worktrees.

10. Lessons log

What we learned while building the reference implementation and running it on the four agent-written changes. Decisions are in the decision log; failure modes in limits and failure modes.

About the problem

# Lesson
L1 Human review "before AI" is not a gold standard. At Vercel, over half of PRs were approved without a comment. Comparing the passport with an idealised human review would flatter the human.
L2 Published auto-approval numbers are self-reported and selection-biased. We cite them as "reported by" and rely on random counter-review for our own measurement.
L3 Check sources before citing them. One vendor blog post we relied on was taken down 48 hours after publication.
L4 No vendor says who answers when an auto-approved change breaks. That gap is exactly what the bounded attestation fills.

About agent-written code

# Lesson
L5 On the 237-file change, the agent was right on the core claim and wrong on two points that break users. Neither its 169 green tests nor a quick read showed it; two tests written from the acceptance criteria showed it in minutes.
L6 An agent's environment claims must be proven. The agent wrote a false platform limitation into code, docs and its own test at once.
L7 Green agent tests measure the agent's belief. Under mutation, its tests noticed 3 of its 10 changes.
L8 The issue text is not evidence either. On one bug fix, the reproduction showed the analyzer crashed, while the issue said it "did not work".

About the AI reviewer

# Lesson
L9 Precise but not complete. On the large change the AI reviewer found 4 issues, all confirmed, missed 1, and was confidently wrong about the one it missed. It directs attention; it does not replace it.
L10 Blind review is useful because it can be checked. Kept away from the evidence, the AI stated in a live run that "no round-trip test exists" while our proof runs one. The verification step caught it; a reviewer that had seen the proofs would have hidden that weakness.
L11 The AI review is not reproducible, so it must never decide. Two live runs on the same change gave different refusals; one moved the lane from orange to red. Because refusals can only raise the lane, the variance costs a review, never an escape.
L12 Severity and confidence stated by the AI are not calibrated. A compile break was rated "low, confidence 0.4". Severity comes from evidence.

About the factory itself

# Lesson
L13 The reading gain comes from rules derived from the requirement, not from the AI. 46 of 1,392 lines on the large change; 39 of 228 and 36 of 266 on the bug fixes. Without traceable claims from the agent, the passport still routes and records, but the owner reads everything.
L14 Verify the factory's own numbers. A path-parsing bug, double-counted lines, a bug injector that changed file encodings and a "green" end-to-end run that exercised nothing were all caught because totals did not add up.
L15 A uniform result is suspicious. 8 of 8 scenarios failing on both sides was our harness, not the code.
L16 A filter against false positives creates false negatives. Test every filter against a known real catch.
L17 Accountability must be enforced by the tool. Writing "the owner signs" is not enough; the signing step itself refuses the wrong signer, a stale commit, unanswered refusals and a red change accepted without a full read.
L18 Our acceptance criteria were written after seeing the changes (hackathon constraint). In production they must be locked before the agent starts, or the passport proves what was delivered rather than what was asked.
